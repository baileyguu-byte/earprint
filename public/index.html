  1. Sub-bass20–60 Hz

    The rumble you feel more than hear: 808s, synth bass and the thump of a kick drum. Small earbuds with a loose fit barely reproduce it.

  2. Bass60–250 Hz

    Punch and weight: kick drums, bass guitar, the low notes of a piano. Too much makes a mix boomy.

  3. Low mids250–500 Hz

    Warmth and body. Too much sounds muddy or boxy; too little sounds thin.

  4. Mids500 Hz–2 kHz

    The core of vocals, guitars and keys. Cutting here makes music sound hollow or distant.

  5. Upper mids2–4 kHz

    Clarity and bite. Ears are most sensitive around here, so small boosts quickly turn harsh.

  6. Presence4–6 kHz

    Definition on voices, snares and guitar picking. A little lift brings vocals forward.

  7. Brilliance and air6–20 kHz

    Cymbals, breath and shimmer. Too much brings out hiss and sharp “s” sounds.

The basics

What an equaliser does

An equaliser (EQ) changes the balance of a song by turning parts of the frequency range up or down. Each slider is a band centred on one frequency, and it also nudges the frequencies either side of it. A 10-band EQ like Earprint’s has one slider per octave, from 32 Hz to 16 kHz.

Changes are measured in decibels (dB). Positive numbers boost, negative numbers cut, and 0 dB leaves that band alone.

Cutting is often cleaner than boosting. If vocals sound buried, try lowering the bass a little before you raise the mids.

+3 dB
A small but clear lift.
+6 dB
A big change. Most good settings stay within this.
±12 dB
Earprint’s limit. Changes this large usually cause more problems than they fix.
Preamp
Boosting can push music past the maximum level and make it distort, which is called clipping. Turning the overall level down by your largest boost leaves room. Earprint’s exports include this.
Louder wins
Played back to back, the louder version almost always sounds better, even if nothing else changed. Earprint matches the volume of the settings you compare, so you judge the tone and not the level.

Three things shape what you hear

Why there’s no one best setting

The recording

Every song has already been balanced by mixing and mastering engineers. A setting that helps one album can spoil another, which is why Earprint lets you test on the exact songs you play.

Your headphones or speakers

Usually the biggest factor. Two pairs of headphones can differ by more than any EQ setting you’d choose, so a setting tuned on one device rarely suits another.

Your ears and your volume

Hearing varies between people and changes with age. Volume matters too: at low levels we hear less bass and treble, so a setting that sounds balanced when loud can sound thin when quiet.

Output devices

Your headphones and speakers change everything

Earprint doesn’t know your device yet, so any setting you find is part taste and part correction for whatever you listened on. Save a separate setting for each device you use.

Earbuds

The seal in your ear decides how much bass you get. Try a different tip size before boosting the bass: a poor fit can lose more low end than any EQ can restore.

Over-ear and on-ear headphones

Every model has its own tonal signature. Open-back designs usually have less deep bass than closed ones, and many headphones have a bright peak somewhere in the treble.

Bluetooth headphones with an app

Many wireless headphones apply their own EQ through a companion app. Stacking that on top of another EQ doubles the effect, so set one of them to flat first.

Speakers and cars

The room or cabin shapes the sound as much as the speakers do. Bass builds up near walls and corners, so a setting made on headphones often sounds bloated on speakers.

Eras and remasters

Artists change over time

An artist’s sound isn’t fixed. Producers, studios and fashions change, and so does the artist. Studies of large collections of commercial recordings have found the overall tonal balance of popular music shifting from decade to decade.

  • Records got louderMastering got much louder through the 1990s and 2000s, with heavier compression. Older records can sound quieter and softer next to newer ones, even at the same EQ setting.
  • Remasters sound differentReissues are often louder and balanced differently from the original release, so the version you stream may not be the one you grew up with.
  • Your favourite era mattersEarprint starts with an artist’s most-played songs, which lean towards their best-known period. If you love an earlier album, search for songs from it and test with those.
  • Test more than one songSongs by the same artist can suit different settings. Try two or three you play most before you settle on one.

Using the lab

From song to setting in four steps

  1. Pick a song

    Search for an artist, song or genre, or drop in your own audio file. Add up to five artists you listen to most.

  2. See where it lives

    The frequency scope shows the song’s sound live, and a chart shows which octaves carry the most energy.

  3. Shape and compare

    Move the ten faders, loop the part of the song you care about, and switch between settings A and B. Run a blind test to choose without bias.

  4. Copy it to your app

    Export for Apple Music on a Mac, Spotify, Equalizer APO on Windows or Wavelet on Android.

Read before you trust the numbers

What Earprint can’t do yet

  • It hears previews, not whole songs.Songs come from Deezer as 30-second, 128 kbps MP3 clips. They cover one part of each song and lose detail above roughly 16 kHz.
  • It doesn’t know your headphones.The settings you find mix your taste with fixes for your device.
  • Your browser isn’t your music app.Every EQ app shapes its bands slightly differently, so exported settings are close approximations. Spotify’s sliders don’t show numbers, so you set them by eye.
  • Volume matching is an estimate.It’s based on each song’s average balance, so very uneven songs can still sound slightly louder on one setting.
  • Some songs have no preview.And some artists appear more than once on Deezer. Earprint shows fan counts so you can pick the main profile.
  • It isn’t a hearing test.If you notice changes in your hearing, see an audiologist or your GP.
  • Keep the volume sensible.Boosting with an EQ makes it easy to listen louder than you realise.
